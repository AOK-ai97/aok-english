Money Lessons They Never Taught Us

Ask a group of eighteen-year-olds how a loan works or what an interest rate is, and many will not know the answer. According to an international survey, only one in three young adults can answer basic questions about money. Yet the same young people will soon be signing phone contracts, renting flats and perhaps taking their first loans.

Part of the problem is that money is rarely discussed at school or at home. In many families, talking about salaries and debts is considered impolite. As a result, young people often learn about money the hard way — by making mistakes.

Online shopping and digital payments have made things more complicated. When we pay with a card or phone, money feels less real than when we hand over cash. Studies have shown that people spend up to thirty per cent more when they pay by card. "Buy now, pay later" services, which are very popular among teenagers, make it even easier to buy things we cannot afford.

Some countries have decided that financial literacy should be taught at school. In the UK, it has been part of the national curriculum since 2014, and in several US states students must pass a personal finance course before they can graduate. Early research suggests that these students are less likely to get into serious debt.

What can young people do on their own? Experts recommend three simple habits: write down every expense for a month to see where the money goes; save a fixed part of every income, even if it is small; and wait 24 hours before buying anything expensive. "If you still want it tomorrow," one adviser says, "then buy it."

2.Why do people spend more when they pay by card?

Teenage Bankers

In 2024 a bank in Tashkent introduced debit cards for teenagers aged 14 to 17. Parents can send pocket money to the card and see every payment online. Supporters say it teaches financial literacy: teenagers learn to plan a budget and avoid impulse buying. Shohruh, 16, has been using the card for a year. 'At first I spent everything in two days,' he admits. 'If I had planned better, I wouldn't have been hungry at school!' Now he saves 20% of his money every month. Critics, however, worry that online banking makes spending too easy, because money doesn't feel real when you don't see it.
