Rule · Qoida
Reported speech — ko'chirma gap
To'g'ri gapKo'chirma gap
Present Simple → Past Simple"I use AI every day." → He said (that) he used AI every day.
Present Continuous → Past Continuous"I'm working." → She said she was working.
Present Perfect / Past → Past Perfect"I've lost my data." → He said he had lost his data.
will → would · can → could"Robots will replace us." → She said robots would replace us.
Savol va buyruq
Ha/Yo'q savol → if / whether"Do you trust AI?" → He asked if I trusted AI.
Wh- savol → so'z tartibi darak"Where do you work?" → She asked where I worked.
Buyruq → told / asked + to"Turn off your phone." → He told me to turn off my phone.
Vaqt so'zlarinow → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there

Will Robots Take Our Jobs?

In 2013, two Oxford researchers published a report that shocked the world. They said that 47 per cent of jobs in the United States could be done by computers within twenty years. Newspapers immediately predicted mass unemployment. Ten years later, however, the picture looks very different.

It is true that automation has replaced many routine jobs. Supermarkets have self-service checkouts, banks need fewer cashiers, and factories use robots to build cars. Yet unemployment in most developed countries is lower today than it was in 2013. Why? Because technology also creates new jobs — many of which did not exist twenty years ago, such as app developers, data analysts and social media managers.

The arrival of artificial intelligence has made the debate more urgent. Unlike earlier machines, AI can write texts, translate languages and even diagnose illnesses. A recent survey found that office workers who used AI tools finished their tasks 40 per cent faster. Some experts warned that jobs in translation, journalism and customer service were now at risk.

Other experts are more optimistic. They argue that AI will not replace people but will change what people do. A doctor who uses AI to analyse X-rays will have more time to talk to patients. A teacher who uses AI to check homework will have more time to help weaker students. In other words, the more routine work machines do, the more time humans have for creative and social tasks.

What should young people do? Most experts agree on the answer: develop skills that machines find difficult, such as creativity, communication and critical thinking — and never stop learning.

Who Is Watching You?

In many cities around the world, thousands of cameras now monitor the streets. Supporters say surveillance has reduced crime: in one city, street robberies declined by thirty percent after cameras were installed. However, critics argue that the benefit comes at a cost — our privacy. Modern systems use artificial intelligence to recognise faces in seconds. Some experts warn that if this data is not regulated, it could be used to control people. A survey in 2024 found that 65% of young people were worried about how their data is used, but only 20% had ever changed their privacy settings.
