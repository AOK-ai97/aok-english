Rule · Qoida
Qarama-qarshilik (B2)
despite / in spite of + ot / V-ingDespite the crowds, we enjoyed Registan. · In spite of being expensive, the tour was worth it.
despite the fact that + gapDespite the fact that it was raining, the square was full.
although / even though + gapEven though tourism brings money, it can damage monuments.
whereas / while — ikki narsani solishtirishBukhara is quiet, whereas Samarkand is busier.
Having said that, — oldingi fikrni yumshatishTourism is useful. Having said that, it must be controlled.

Reading · O'qish · Reading Part 3 format
Choose the correct heading for each paragraph (1–6) from A–H. There are two extra headings. — 6 abzats — 8 sarlavha
Too Many Tourists?

1. Venice, a city of 50,000 residents, receives more than 20 million visitors a year. On a summer day, the narrow streets are so crowded that locals can hardly walk to work. Many residents have left, and the city has been called a "theme park" rather than a living town.

2. Venice is not alone. From Barcelona to Bali, popular places are struggling with the same problem. Rents have risen because flats are rented to tourists, and traditional shops have been replaced by souvenir stalls selling the same cheap products.

3. Old monuments suffer too. Millions of feet wear down ancient stone floors, and the breath of thousands of visitors changes the temperature and humidity inside old buildings, damaging paintings and tiles.

4. Some cities have started to fight back. Venice now charges day-trippers an entrance fee, Amsterdam has banned new souvenir shops in the centre, and the famous Machu Picchu in Peru allows only a limited number of visitors per day, who must book in advance.

5. For Uzbekistan, where tourism is growing fast, these examples are important lessons. Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva welcomed millions of visitors last year, and the number is rising. Tourism brings income and jobs, especially for hotels, restaurants and local craftspeople.

6. The challenge is to grow without losing authenticity. Experts suggest spreading tourists across the year and across the country, developing eco-tourism in the mountains, and involving local communities in decisions. Despite the difficulties, they believe a balance is possible.

A City Inside Walls

Itchan Kala, the old town of Khiva, became Uzbekistan's first UNESCO site in 1990. Inside its ancient walls there are more than fifty monuments, including mosques, madrasahs and palaces. Many local families still live there, which makes the town feel authentic. Despite its popularity, Khiva is far less crowded than Samarkand, especially in autumn. However, the number of visitors has doubled in the last five years. Some residents are happy because tourism has increased their income, whereas others complain that the old streets are becoming noisy and that souvenir shops are replacing traditional workshops.

1.Itchan Kala became a UNESCO site in 1990.
2.There are fewer than twenty monuments.
3.Nobody lives in the old town now.
4.The number of tourists has doubled.
5.All residents are happy with tourism.
