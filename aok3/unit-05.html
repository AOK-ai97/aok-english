Rule · Qoida
Relative clauses — aniqlovchi ergash gaplar
who — odamThe doctor who helped me was very kind.
which / that — narsaFood which is high in sugar is bad for teeth.
whose — egalikA boy whose diet is poor gets tired quickly.
where — joyThis is the café where they serve vegan food.
Defining va Non-defining
Defining (vergulsiz) — kerakli ma'lumotPeople who eat fast food every day risk obesity.
Non-defining (vergul bilan) — qo'shimcha ma'lumot, that ishlatilmaydiPlov, which is our national dish, is quite fatty.

The Truth About Sugar

Fifty years ago, most doctors believed that fat was the main enemy of a healthy diet. Supermarkets filled their shelves with "low-fat" yoghurts, biscuits and drinks. What few people noticed was that, to keep these products tasty, manufacturers added large amounts of sugar instead. Today many scientists think this was one of the biggest mistakes in the history of nutrition.

The World Health Organization recommends that adults eat no more than 25 grams — about six teaspoons — of added sugar a day. Yet the average teenager in many countries eats three times that amount. A single can of cola contains around 35 grams, which is already more than the daily limit.

Sugar is not only found in sweets. It is hidden in products that most people don't think of as sweet, such as ketchup, bread, sauces and even some "healthy" breakfast cereals. Reading labels is the only way to know what we are really eating, but the information is often printed in tiny letters, and sugar may appear under names like glucose, fructose or syrup.

Too much sugar has been linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes and tooth decay. Some studies also suggest that diets which are high in sugar may affect mood and concentration, although more research is needed.

Several countries have decided to act. Mexico, the UK and others have introduced a "sugar tax" on sweet drinks. In the UK, many companies reduced the sugar in their drinks before the tax even started, because they did not want to pay it. Critics, however, say that such taxes punish poor families, who spend a larger part of their income on food.

Nutritionists agree that we don't need to remove sugar completely. A piece of cake at a birthday party is not a problem. The key, as with most things, is moderation — and knowing where sugar is hiding.

Reading the Label

Last month, Dilnoza decided to check the labels on the food that her family buys. She was shocked. The 'healthy' juice which her children drank every day contained eight spoonfuls of sugar per bottle. The breakfast cereal, whose box showed happy children playing sport, was 30% sugar. 'I wish someone had told me earlier,' she says. Now Dilnoza cooks more homemade food and buys fewer processed products. Her family eats more vegetables and fibre, and portions are smaller. After a month, her son, who used to feel tired all the time, has much more energy.

1.Dilnoza checked the food labels.
2.The juice contained a lot of sugar.
3.The cereal was 50% sugar.
4.Her husband is a doctor.
