Read the text and answer the questions on the next page. — matn — savollar keyingi sahifada
The Doctor Will See You Now — Online

Before 2020, telemedicine was a niche service used mainly in remote regions where doctors were scarce. Within months of the pandemic, it had become mainstream. Governments relaxed regulations almost overnight, allowing doctors to prescribe medicines after a video call. In some countries, the proportion of consultations carried out by video or phone rose from under 1 per cent to over 40 per cent. Although the figures have since fallen, they remain far above pre-pandemic levels.

The advantages for patients are considerable. Online consultations eliminate travel time and reduce waiting, which is particularly valuable for elderly patients, people with disabilities and those living far from hospitals. For doctors, telemedicine allows a more efficient organisation of the working day, and for health systems under financial pressure, it offers a potential reduction in costs.

Mental health services have benefited in particular. Many patients find it easier to talk about anxiety or depression from the privacy of their own home, and some studies suggest that online therapy can be as effective as face-to-face sessions for certain conditions.

However, the shift has raised concerns. Physical examination remains essential for the accurate diagnosis of many conditions, and some doctors worry that serious illnesses may be missed when they cannot see or touch the patient. A study of skin conditions, for example, found that diagnoses made from photographs differed from in-person diagnoses in roughly one case in five.

There are also questions of equality. Ironically, those who could benefit most from remote consultations — older and poorer patients — are often the least likely to own a smartphone or have reliable internet access. Language barriers and poor hearing can make video calls especially difficult for older patients. Without investment in digital literacy and infrastructure, telemedicine could widen rather than narrow existing health inequalities.

Most experts now favour a hybrid model in which routine follow-ups, prescription renewals and mental health support are offered online, while first consultations and complex cases are handled in person. The challenge for policymakers is to ensure that convenience does not come at the expense of quality.

Doctors on Screen

The introduction of telemedicine has transformed healthcare access in rural Uzbekistan. Before its expansion, the nearest specialist for many villages was over a hundred kilometres away, and a single consultation could mean a day's journey and lost income. Now, a nurse in a local clinic can connect a patient with a cardiologist in Tashkent within minutes. The reduction in travel time has been significant, and early diagnosis of chronic diseases such as diabetes has improved. However, the system has limitations. A lack of reliable internet in mountainous areas remains a barrier, and some elderly patients express distrust of consultations conducted through a screen. Furthermore, a physical examination cannot always be replaced.
