Rule · Qoida
Cleft sentences — ma'lum qismga urg'u berish
It is / was + X + that / who …It was the internet that changed global trade.
It is young people who benefit most.
What + S + V + is / was …What worries me is the loss of local culture.
The thing / reason (why) … is …The reason why prices fell is that production moved abroad.
It was not until … that …It was not until the 1990s that globalisation really accelerated.
All (that) … is / was …All we need is fair trade.

Reading · O'qish · Reading Part 3 format
Choose the correct heading for each paragraph (1–6) from A–H. There are two extra headings. — 6 abzats — 8 sarlavha
One World, Many Questions

1. Take a typical smartphone. Its design may come from California, its chips from Taiwan, its screen from South Korea, its rare metals from Africa, and it may be assembled in China before being sold in Tashkent. Few products illustrate the interdependence of the modern world so vividly. Even a simple cotton T-shirt may cross several borders before it reaches a shop.

2. Supporters of globalisation point out that, over the past forty years, global trade has helped lift more than a billion people out of extreme poverty. Countries that opened their economies, such as South Korea and Vietnam, have seen dramatic rises in living standards. Cheap imported goods have also allowed ordinary families to buy products that were once luxuries.

3. Yet the benefits have not been shared equally. In many wealthy countries, factory jobs disappeared as production moved to places with cheaper labour, leaving entire towns in decline. It is this sense of being left behind that has fuelled much of the recent political backlash against free trade.

4. Culture, too, has been transformed. Hollywood films, K-pop and global fast-food chains are familiar from Lagos to Samarkand. Critics fear a world in which every high street looks the same, while others argue that cultures have always borrowed from each other and continue to adapt rather than disappear. Uzbek plov, for instance, is now served in restaurants from Moscow to New York.

5. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed a further weakness. When factories in one country closed, shortages of everything from medical masks to car parts rippled across the globe. What many governments learnt is that long supply chains, though efficient, can be fragile.

6. As a result, some economists now speak of "slowbalisation": a world that remains connected but in which companies keep production closer to home and countries protect key industries. For consumers, this may mean slightly higher prices but more reliable supplies. Whether this trend will last remains to be seen.

The New Silk Road

For centuries, cities such as Samarkand and Bukhara grew wealthy as stops on the Silk Road, where goods, ideas and religions travelled between China and Europe. Today, a new version of this network is being built. Railways, highways and pipelines now link Central Asia to global markets, attracting billions in foreign investment. What supporters emphasise is the creation of jobs and the transfer of technology. Critics, however, point to the risk of debt: several countries have borrowed heavily to finance infrastructure. It is not only economics that is at stake, but also local identity, as foreign products and cultural influences arrive faster than ever. Whether the new Silk Road will bring the prosperity of the old one remains to be seen.

1.Samarkand and Bukhara were stops on the Silk Road.
2.The new network includes railways and pipelines.
3.Critics are worried about debt.
4.All Central Asian countries have avoided borrowing.
5.The writer is sure the new Silk Road will succeed.
