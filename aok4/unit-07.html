Can a Machine Be an Artist?

In 2022, a picture called Théâtre D'opéra Spatial won first prize in the digital art category of a competition in the United States. Nothing unusual about that — except that the picture had been created with the help of an AI program. The artist, Jason Allen, had typed hundreds of instructions into the software and then edited the results. When it was revealed that the (1) had been produced by AI, many artists were furious. Allen himself defended his work, explaining that he had spent more than eighty hours creating it. Some said a line had been crossed; others argued that the (2) of art itself was changing. The debate raises old questions. Art has always reflected the nature of its time. When photography was invented in the nineteenth century, painters feared that the camera would make them unnecessary. Instead, photography became a new form of art, and painting moved in new directions. Supporters of AI argue that the software is simply another tool, like the (3) . Critics reply that there is an important difference: a photographer still chooses what to photograph, whereas AI programs learn by copying millions of images made by human (4) , often without permission or payment. Some artists have even taken legal action against technology companies, claiming that their work was used to train AI systems. Others, however, have embraced the new tools and use them to generate ideas quickly before painting by hand. Museums and galleries are now facing similar questions. With regard to the law, many countries have not yet decided who owns AI-generated images. Some courts have ruled that only works created by humans can be protected. Whatever the legal answer, one thing seems clear: the (5) between human and machine creativity is becoming harder to define. Perhaps the more important question is not whether a machine can be an (6) , but what we want art to do for us.

Who Owns a Painting Made by AI?

In 2022, an image produced with an AI program won first prize in an art competition in the United States, provoking outrage among artists. The creator argued that writing the instructions required skill and creativity; critics countered that the machine had done the actual work. The debate has since intensified. In terms of law, most countries do not grant copyright to works created without significant human input. With regard to ethics, many illustrators point out that AI systems were trained on millions of images without their permission or payment. Owing to these concerns, some galleries have banned AI-generated works, whereas others have opened exhibitions dedicated to them. What remains uncertain is whether audiences value the process behind a work or only the final result.
