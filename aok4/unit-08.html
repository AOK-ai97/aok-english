Rule · Qoida
Reporting passive — ehtiyotkor xabar berish (C1)
It is said / believed / alleged that + gapIt is alleged that the minister lied.
S + is said / thought / believed to + V1The president is said to be ill.
S + is said to have + V3 (o'tmish)The journalist is believed to have left the country.
S + is reported to be + V-ingTalks are reported to be continuing.

✔ Jurnalistlar bu shakldan foydalanadi, chunki ma'lumot hali isbotlanmagan bo'ladi.

Reading · O'qish · Reading Part 3 format
Choose the correct heading for each paragraph (1–6) from A–H. There are two extra headings. — 6 abzats — 8 sarlavha
How Far Should Journalists Go?

1. In 2011, a British newspaper that had existed for 168 years closed down almost overnight. It was revealed that its journalists had illegally listened to the private voicemail messages of celebrities, politicians and even crime victims. The scandal led to a public inquiry and a lasting debate about how far journalists should be allowed to go. Several senior editors were arrested, and the owner publicly apologised to the victims.

2. Investigative journalism has a proud history. Reporters have exposed corruption, environmental disasters and abuses of power that would otherwise have remained hidden. Famous investigations have even forced presidents and prime ministers to resign. Without the freedom to dig into uncomfortable stories, democracy itself would arguably be weaker.

3. The difficulty lies in drawing the line between the public interest and what merely interests the public. A politician's secret financial deals are clearly the public's business; the details of a celebrity's divorce arguably are not, however many readers they attract. Editors must therefore ask whether a story serves a genuine public purpose before publishing it.

4. The digital age has made these questions more urgent. Online, every article competes for clicks, and sensational headlines tend to attract far more attention than careful reporting. Clickbait, critics argue, rewards speed over accuracy and outrage over understanding.

5. Meanwhile, anyone with a smartphone can now act as a journalist. Citizen journalists have filmed events that professional media could not reach, but they rarely follow professional codes of conduct, and their footage can be shared millions of times before anyone checks whether it is genuine. During natural disasters, for example, the first images often come from ordinary people rather than reporters.

6. Some argue for stricter regulation; others warn that governments could use such laws to silence critics. Most media experts favour independent regulators, transparent corrections and, above all, better media literacy among readers themselves.

The Price of a Headline

In 2011, a major British tabloid closed after it emerged that its journalists had illegally accessed the phone messages of celebrities, politicians and even crime victims. The newspaper was said to have sold millions of copies thanks to stories obtained in this way. The scandal led to a public inquiry, which examined the culture and ethics of the British press. Supporters of strict regulation argued that self-regulation had clearly failed. Opponents warned that government control over journalism would threaten freedom of the press, which is considered essential to holding the powerful to account. More than a decade later, the balance between accountability and press freedom remains disputed, especially in the age of clickbait and citizen journalism.
