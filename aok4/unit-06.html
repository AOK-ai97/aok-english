Every Drop Counts

Central Asia is often described as a region rich in energy but poor in water. In Uzbekistan, agriculture accounts for roughly ninety per cent of all water use, most of it delivered to fields through open canals built during the Soviet era. Experts estimate that up to half of this water may be lost through evaporation and leakage before it reaches the crops.

The consequences are well known. Rivers that once fed the Aral Sea now dry up long before they reach it, and the region's population — and therefore its demand for food and water — continues to grow. Climate change appears to be making the situation worse: glaciers in the mountains, which act as natural water towers, are shrinking, and summers are becoming hotter. In addition, much of the irrigated land has become salty, because water evaporates and leaves minerals behind, which reduces harvests year after year.

Yet solutions exist. Drip irrigation, which delivers water directly to the roots of plants through thin pipes, can reduce water consumption by thirty to fifty per cent while increasing yields. Since 2019, the Uzbek government has offered subsidies to farmers who install such systems, and the area under efficient irrigation has expanded rapidly, although it still represents only a small share of the total. Laser levelling of fields, which makes the ground perfectly flat so that water spreads evenly, is another relatively cheap technique that is being introduced.

Another approach is to change what is grown. Cotton and rice are particularly thirsty crops. Some farmers have switched to fruit, vegetables and nuts, which tend to require less water and are often more profitable on export markets.

Nevertheless, technology alone is unlikely to be sufficient. Water is still very cheap for many users, which arguably gives them little incentive to save it. Many analysts conclude that a combination of investment, fair pricing and regional cooperation between upstream and downstream countries will be required to secure the region's water future. After all, neighbouring countries depend on the same rivers, so decisions taken upstream inevitably affect farmers downstream.

Sun and Water

Uzbekistan appears to be well positioned for an energy transition: it receives over 300 sunny days a year, and large areas of desert are suitable for solar farms. Since 2020, several international companies have invested in solar and wind projects, and the share of renewables in electricity generation is expected to rise significantly. However, the transition faces considerable challenges. The country's electricity grid is ageing, and blackouts during cold winters suggest that energy storage remains insufficient. Additionally, subsidies have kept prices low, which tends to discourage efficient consumption. Water presents a further dilemma: hydropower depends on rivers that are also vital for irrigation, and as droughts become more frequent, these demands may increasingly conflict.

2.International companies have invested in renewables.

4.Low prices encourage people to save energy.
5.Hydropower and irrigation may compete for water.
