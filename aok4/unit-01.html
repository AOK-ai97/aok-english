Rule · Qoida
Inversiya — urg'u uchun (C1)
Not only + aux + S … but alsoNot only does the reform cost less, but it also works better.
Never / Rarely / Seldom + aux + SRarely have students been so motivated.
Hardly / Scarcely … whenHardly had the lesson begun when the lights went out.
No sooner … thanNo sooner had the law passed than schools changed.
Only when / Only after + clause, aux + SOnly when teachers are trained will the reform succeed.
Under no circumstances + aux + SUnder no circumstances should exams replace learning.

What Finland Got Right

For two decades, Finland's education system has been the subject of international fascination. Its students consistently perform well in global assessments, yet they start school at seven, have shorter school days than most of their European peers, and take almost no standardised tests until the end of secondary school. How can a system that seems to ask so little achieve so much?

Part of the answer lies in the status of teachers. In Finland, teaching is among the most respected professions, and competition for places on teacher-training programmes is fierce: only about one in ten applicants is accepted. All teachers must hold a master's degree, and once qualified they enjoy considerable freedom to design their own lessons. Not only are they trusted, but they are also rarely inspected.

Equality is another cornerstone. Almost all Finnish children attend state schools, and the difference in results between the best and worst schools is among the smallest in the world. Extra support is provided early and quietly to pupils who fall behind, so that very few drop out.

Another feature that visitors often notice is the amount of free time. Finnish pupils have a fifteen-minute break after every lesson, and they are encouraged to spend it outside, whatever the weather. Teachers believe that children who move regularly concentrate better, and homework is kept to a minimum, especially in primary school. Not only does this approach reduce stress, but it also leaves time for hobbies, sport and family life.

Critics, however, warn against treating Finland as a miracle that can simply be copied. The country is small, relatively wealthy and culturally homogeneous, and its results have actually declined somewhat since 2006. Some Finnish educators themselves argue that the system has been romanticised abroad, and that the mobile-phone era poses challenges that no curriculum has yet solved.

Nevertheless, several lessons appear transferable. Investing in teacher quality, reducing high-stakes testing and providing support before problems become serious are policies that have improved results elsewhere, from Estonia to parts of Canada. Only when such reforms are adapted to local conditions, rather than imported wholesale, do they seem to succeed. Policymakers elsewhere would therefore be wise to study not only Finland's results but also the social conditions that made them possible.

2.What is true about Finnish teachers?
3.Why do few Finnish students drop out?

The Finnish Puzzle

For two decades, Finland has been held up as a model of educational success. Not only do Finnish students perform well in international assessments, but they also spend fewer hours in class than most of their European peers. Formal schooling does not begin until the age of seven, and standardised tests are almost entirely absent until the end of secondary school. Critics, however, warn against simply copying the system. Finland is a small, wealthy and relatively equal society, and its teachers are drawn from the top ten percent of graduates. Rarely is the prestige of teaching so high elsewhere. Moreover, Finland's own results have declined since 2012, prompting a national debate about whether its celebrated reforms still work.

2.Formal schooling starts at seven.
3.Finnish teachers are well paid compared to doctors.
4.Finland's results have fallen since 2012.
5.The writer believes other countries should copy Finland exactly.
